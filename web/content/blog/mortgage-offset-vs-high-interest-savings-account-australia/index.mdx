If you hold a variable-rate Australian home loan and keep a cash buffer: an emergency fund, sinking funds for quarterly bills, or savings for a renovation — that money is quietly making a decision for you. Every dollar sitting in a savings account earns taxable interest. Every dollar sitting in a **100% mortgage offset account** earns nothing on paper but saves you mortgage interest that is never taxed, because it was never income.

The comparison is not close in most cases. Here is the after-tax maths, the situations where a savings account genuinely wins, and how to model your own numbers.

## The Core Insight: Saved Interest Is Untaxed

Interest you *earn* is assessable income taxed at your marginal rate (plus the 2% Medicare levy). Interest you *save* is not income at all. It is an expense that never existed.

At a 32% marginal rate, your offset needs to beat the *after-tax* savings rate, which on a 4.5% HISA is only about 3.06%. Since variable home loan rates currently sit well above that, the offset wins on every dollar, every day the cash sits there.

## The Compounding Bonus Most Comparisons Miss

The year-one comparison understates the offset benefit. Every dollar of interest not charged flows into principal reduction, which shrinks the interest base for the following month. A persistent offset balance shortens the loan itself.

On a $650,000 loan at 5.9% with 25 years remaining, a persistent $40,000 offset saves roughly **$2,360 in avoided interest in year one alone**. Because the loan repays sooner, the lifetime saving runs into the tens of thousands of dollars. ## When a High-Interest Savings Account Actually Wins

Offset is not universally better. A HISA (or term deposit) can be the right home for cash when:

- **You have no home loan, or a fixed-rate loan.** Most fixed-rate Australian mortgages either lack an offset facility entirely or only allow a partial offset (for example, 40% of the balance). A partial offset cuts the effective benefit proportionally.
- **Your offset product charges a premium.** Some loans add a higher rate or a monthly package fee for offset features. If the fee exceeds roughly $10–$15/month per $10,000 of average offset balance, a free HISA can beat it.
- **The cash is not really spare.** If there is a real chance you will spend the buffer on a holiday, keeping it psychologically "out of sight" in a separate savings account can be worth the small rate sacrifice.
- **You are on a low marginal rate.** Below the tax-free threshold and effective LITO zone, the tax cost of HISA interest shrinks toward zero, though your loan rate would still usually beat the gross HISA rate anyway.

<Callout type="tip">
Check whether your "offset" is genuine. Some products marketed as offset are actually **redraw facilities**: extra repayments you can pull back, subject to bank approval, minimum amounts, and processing delays. A genuine 100% offset is a separate transaction account with instant access. The distinction matters for an emergency fund.
</Callout>

## Offset vs Redraw vs Paying Down the Loan

Putting the same $40,000 *inside* the loan as extra repayments saves the same interest as offsetting it, but the money stops being yours to access freely. Redraw usually requires a request, can carry minimums, and is not guaranteed.

| Approach | Interest saved | Access to cash | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **100% offset account** | Same as repaying | Instant (it is your transaction account) | None beyond normal deposit risk |
| **Extra repayments + redraw** | Same as offset | Delayed, conditional | Bank can restrict or remove redraw |
| **HISA / term deposit** | None — taxed interest instead | Instant / locked | Rate risk on rollover |

For investors, there is a second-order consideration: parking personal cash in an offset against an **investment-property** loan preserves deductible interest if you later convert the property, whereas paying it down permanently reduces future deductible debt. That is a structural decision worth discussing with an accountant.
