Every July, millions of Australians sit down with twelve months of bank statements and an inbox search bar, trying to remember whether a $189 Officeworks charge from November was a printer monitor arm for work or back-to-school supplies. By the time you lodge your tax return—either yourself via myTax before 31 October or through a registered tax agent—faded thermal receipts and missing invoices mean legitimate deductions get left on the table.

Here is how Australian tax substantiation actually works, why a bank statement alone is rarely enough, and how to set up a lightweight workflow that keeps your deduction evidence attached to the expense all year.

<Callout type="note">
This guide explains record-keeping mechanics and Australian Taxation Office (ATO) substantiation rules for general information. It is not personal tax advice—always confirm deduction eligibility with the ATO or a registered tax agent.
</Callout>

## The Three Rules Every Work-Related Deduction Must Pass

Before worrying about apps or folders, every work-related expense claim for an Australian employee rests on three core tests:

1. **You spent the money yourself and were not reimbursed** by your employer.
2. **The expense directly relates to earning your assessable income**: it is not private, domestic, or capital in nature.
3. **You have a record to prove it** (substantiation).

Most people trip up on the third rule: not because they didn't buy the item, but because they kept the wrong type of record or lost the apportionment calculation between work and private use.

## Why a Bank Statement Line Is Usually Not Enough

A common misconception is that downloading a CSV from your bank at the end of June satisfies the ATO's record-keeping rules. In most cases, a bank or credit card statement only proves **that** you paid a merchant on a date; it does not prove **what** you bought (the same gap explains why [bank statement imports work differently from open banking feeds](/blog/bank-statement-import-vs-open-banking-australia/) — neither carries itemised tax-invoice detail).

To substantiate a work-related expense, written evidence from a supplier generally needs to show five details:

| Required Detail | On a Tax Invoice / Receipt | On a Bank Statement Line |
|---|---|---|
| Name of the supplier | Yes | Usually (often truncated) |
| Amount of the expense | Yes (including GST breakdown) | Total only |
| Nature of the goods or services | Yes (itemized line items) | **No** |
| Date the expense was incurred | Yes | Settlement date only |
| Date of the document | Yes | Statement period |

If your total claim for work-related expenses exceeds **$300** (excluding car expenses, meal allowances, award transport payments, and travel allowance expenses), you must have written evidence for **all** of your claims—not just the amount above $300.

## Organizing Expenses by ATO Deduction Categories (D1 to D15)

Instead of dumping every receipt into a single "Tax" folder, tagging expenses against the actual schedule categories used on the Australian individual tax return makes year-end review much faster (our [occupation deductions guide](/blog/occupation-tax-deductions-guide-engineers-nurses-teachers-tradies/) shows which categories matter most for engineers, nurses, teachers, and tradies):

- **D1 — Work-related car expenses**: Kilometres travelled for work between workplaces or client sites (excluding normal home-to-work commuting), using either the cents-per-kilometre method (up to 5,000 business km per car) or a 12-week logbook.
- **D2 — Work-related travel expenses**: Public transport, flights, overnight accommodation, and meals on work trips where you sleep away from home.
- **D3 — Work-related clothing, laundry, and dry-cleaning**: Occupation-specific clothing, protective gear (steel-capped boots, high-vis), or compulsory registered uniforms—never conventional business attire.
- **D4 — Work-related self-education expenses**: Courses, textbooks, and student union fees that maintain or improve specific skills required in your *current* employment.
- **D5 — Other work-related expenses**: Working from home running expenses, union fees, professional memberships, trade tools, technical subscriptions, and work-related portions of phone or internet bills.
- **D10 — Cost of managing tax affairs**: Registered tax agent fees, accounting software, and travel to your tax advisor.
- **D15 — Gifts or donations**: Donations of $2 or more to Deductible Gift Recipients (DGRs) where you receive no material benefit (such as raffle tickets or chocolates) in return.
- **Income protection insurance**: Premiums paid for policies held *outside* of superannuation that replace your salary if you are unable to work due to illness or injury.

## Handling Mixed Personal and Work Expenses

Very few modern expenses are 100% work-related. You might use your home NBN connection, mobile phone plan, or laptop for both work and personal streaming.

When an expense is partly private and partly work-related, you need two things attached to the record:

1. **The supplier invoice or receipt** showing the total cost.
2. **A reasonable apportionment basis**, such as a representative four-week diary showing what percentage of use was for work (for example, 40% work use on an $85/month mobile plan = $34/month deductible).

## The Five-Year Retention Rule

For most individual taxpayers, the ATO requires you to keep your written evidence for **five years** from the date you lodge your tax return. If you claim decline in value (depreciation) on a work asset over several years—such as a $2,400 laptop—the five-year clock starts from the date of your *last* claim for decline in value on that asset.
